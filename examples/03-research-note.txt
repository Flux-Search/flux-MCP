Coastal seagrass recovering faster than expected, survey finds

Seagrass meadows along the Kestrel Bay coast have regrown by about a third since 2021, according to a survey published this week by the Marine Habitats Trust, a pace its authors describe as faster than any model had predicted.

The trust mapped 412 hectares of seagrass in the bay last summer, up from 309 hectares four years earlier. Most of the new growth was in sheltered inlets where mooring buoys had been replaced with floating designs that do not drag along the seabed.

Dr Amara Lindqvist, who led the survey, said the moorings explained most of the change. "Where the old chains came out, the meadows came back within two seasons," she said. "Where they stayed, almost nothing changed."

The finding matters beyond the bay. Seagrass stores carbon in the sediment beneath it, and the trust estimates the new meadows will hold around 9,000 tonnes of carbon once they are established. It also shelters young fish; the survey counted twice as many juvenile pollack in restored inlets as in unrestored ones.

Not all of the coast improved. Two sites near the harbour mouth lost ground, which Lindqvist put down to dredging carried out the previous winter. The trust has asked the harbour authority to share its dredging plans so that future work avoids the remaining beds.

The next full survey is planned for 2028. In the meantime the trust will fit floating moorings at a further 40 sites, paid for by a grant from the regional water company announced last month.
